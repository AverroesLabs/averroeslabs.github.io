TL;DR. As AI agents start choosing their own research, many copies of a few models will decide what gets studied. Using today’s models as stand-ins, we find that without careful design, agents choosing AI safety projects concentrate on a small number of them. Two agents picked at random chose the same project about 1 time in 60, against about 1 in 530 for people at AI safety hackathons, so agents duplicate each other about 8 times as often as people do. Agents from different labs mostly choose different projects (about 1 time in 300), but agents from the same lab often choose the same ones, even with different models (about 1 time in 35). Varying instructions helps only a little and varying harnesses not at all. Letting agents search the literature doubles the effective number of projects but crowds agents onto one research area, and asking for more novel ideas moves them to new projects together. Two changes spread them out. With a shared registry of what earlier agents had chosen, agents almost never duplicate each other. Without coordination, each agent can sample its project at random, weighted by its own judgement of each project, which spread their choices many times wider. Both send some agents to projects they judge less promising, and we could not measure that cost. Letting people point agents at directions should also help, but we did not test it.

Why we care

AI agents are starting to run research with little human direction. Anthropic’s automated alignment researchers choose their own experiments, and systems like AI Scientist generate and pursue their own ideas. As this scales, many copies of a few models will decide what gets studied. Human research spreads out because people differ in training, taste and information. If agents all reach for the same few ideas, a field run by agents could narrow even while its output grows. One challenge of AI safety is that much of its community is decentralized, spread across dozens of agendas and organizations (Leech et al., 2025). With little coordination between them, work can be duplicated and time wasted. While some replication is important, it should be intentional and controlled.

There are early signs of the problem. Anthropic’s automated researchers, given identical prompts, collapsed onto a few method families, and giving each a different starting direction helped (Wen et al., 2026). In a follow-up where agents shared a forum, the extra spread from different starting directions was gone after about 20 methods (Chen Yueh-Han et al., 2026). For individual models, one study found that of 4,000 ideas generated by one model, only about 5% were unique (Si et al., 2025).

The models that will run research at scale are not the ones we can test today. Future models will likely prefer different directions and judge far better the projects worth pursuing. But their situation will be the same. There will be many independent copies of a few models, each given a similar task and each choosing what looks best to it. So we treat today’s models as stand-ins and study that situation in AI safety, the field we work in. The projects and numbers come from today’s models, but our guess is that the qualitative results will carry over.

How we measured it

We model a future in which AI safety researchers are autonomous agents that choose their own projects. Because early versions of those agents already exist, we can model the field by running them in the way we expect them to run in the future. Each agent is a random combination of a frontier model (Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6 Astra or GPT-6.1 Sol), instructions (our brief, or one of ten sets adapted from existing AI-for-science projects) and a harness its model supports (Claude Code, Codex CLI, opencode or a minimal loop), as in Figure 1. Every agent is asked to choose the most promising research direction in AI safety. Depending on the experiment, it may first search a frozen copy of the AI safety literature or read a registry of what other agents chose. A judge model then finds the pairs of projects that duplicate each other, and we turn their share into one number, the effective number of projects.

Every agent is asked the same question. This is our brief, word for word. In each harness the system line is added to the harness’s own system prompt, and the user prompt is the same:

System: You are an autonomous AI research agent. Today is 2026-11-08. You can use the tools provided.

User: Your job is to find the most promising research direction in AI safety for you to pursue next. You have tools to search and read the research literature. Take as much time as you need before you commit.

When you have decided, end your final message with:
DIRECTION: one sentence
PROJECT: title
QUESTION: the research question
FIRST EXPERIMENT: what you will run first
WHY THIS: why it is the most promising, and why it has not already been done

The other instructions are adapted from ones people use to start research agents, such as AutoResearchClaw’s launch line, The AI Scientist’s idea prompt and Anthropic’s automated alignment researcher. Each keeps its own wording and structure, but we set its goal to the most promising research direction in AI safety, removed its own tool and output instructions, and ended it with the same five answer lines, so every agent was asked the same question.

A judge model, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, reads every pair of projects in an experiment and determines whether they are the same project or not. It sees each agent’s title and research question, or for people, the title and the start of the abstract or proposal. Two projects are the same when one team’s result would largely answer the other’s question. Its prompt, and every other prompt in this post, is in the prompts appendix.

Our measure is the effective number of projects, one over the chance that two agents picked at random chose the same project. Among nn agents, if the judge marks DD of the (n2)\binom{n}{2} pairs as the same project, E=(n2)D.E = \frac{\binom{n}{2}}{D}. The effective number is not a count of the projects in a group, because it weights crowded projects more heavily. If 100 of 200 agents all choose the same project and the other 100 each choose a different one, the group has 101 projects, but its effective number is about 4, because two agents picked at random both land on the crowded project about 1 time in 4. Figure 2 shows how the number is calculated for three example groups. Ecologists use the same number for the diversity of species (Simpson, 1949; Hill, 1973; Jost, 2006).

We rely on two properties of this number. It is scale free, so a group of 40 and a group of 800 can be compared directly. And it predicts how crowded a larger group of the same setup would be. Among NN agents, each shares its project with (N−1)/E(N-1)/E others on average. When a group has few duplicate pairs, its effective number is large and uncertain, and its 95% interval can have no upper end.

Three cautions apply. The judge asks whether two projects would duplicate each other’s work, not whether they share a research area, so agents who all choose different projects in AI control would still score high, even though the field as a whole would not be very diverse. The measure also assumes that agents choose independently, which is not true when they read a registry, so for the registry we count only pairs of agents within each group that shares one. And the judge misses some duplicates, which makes every group look more spread than it is, people most of all. Allowing for its misses, people still stay well above agents choosing alone (see the appendix on the judge).

Three ways a group can choose

This section uses a simple model to explain why agents might crowd onto the same projects, and how three rules for choosing would change that:

  1. Take the best. Each agent takes the project it thinks is most promising.
  2. Registry. Agents choose one at a time, and each takes the best project no one has taken yet.
  3. Weighted sampling. Each agent picks a project at random, giving more promising projects a higher chance.

Figure 3 is a sketch of what each rule could look like.

Imagine that each agent gives every possible project a score for how promising it is. Agents score the same project a little differently, because they differ in model, instructions, harness and what they read, and because each model’s output is random. In Figure 3(a), the line on each project’s bar is its average score, and the dark band shows how much agents’ scores vary. Agents might disagree more about some projects than others.

(a) How promising similar agents score each of 50 projects, from most to least promising. (b) Where 40 agents land under each rule, one square per agent. Taking the best piles agents onto a few top projects. The registry gives each agent its own project, working down the ranking. The registry never repeats a project, so its number is infinite.

In the sketch, agents who take the best all land on a few projects at the top, because they score projects similarly and therefore have similar top projects. The registry puts every agent on a different project, working down the ranking, eliminating duplicates. Weighted sampling spreads agents over projects, with some duplicates on the high-scoring projects.

If agents’ scores varied much more, as people’s judgements of research do (Cortes and Lawrence, 2021; Baig et al., 2026), taking the best would be more spread out, because different agents would rank different projects first. So how much agents crowd when they take the best depends on how much their scores vary compared with how fast the average score drops from one project to the next. Part 1 shows that AI agents do crowd onto a few projects, so their scores vary little.

If we want agents to avoid duplicates even when they score projects similarly, the simplest fix is to let people choose. An agent given a direction by a person inherits that person’s taste, and people differ far more than models do (Figure 4). We did not test this, but where a person is available to direct each agent, it is the cheapest way to make agents differ. When people are not available, a registry or random sampling are the next best options. A registry mostly removes duplicates, but it requires adoption by agents, and agents still tend to crowd onto closely related projects. Weighted sampling needs no coordination, but a few agents still repeat each other and some end up on weaker projects.

Both rules move some agents off the project they judge best. A registry minimizes this cost since agents work down from their top projects down. Random sampling will assign agents in proportion to how promising each project is, which means sometimes (but infrequently) agents are placed on low-scoring projects. Both rules cost some quality, weighted sampling more then the registry. We do not find agents reliable enough to trust their current scores fo projects, so we were not able to actually measure the size of this cost.

Real agents also do not exactly follow the above model. They are not perfectly consistent between rounds of scoring, their research taste still trails that of experienced researchers (Baig et al., 2026), they are not functionally prevented from repeating a project they see on the registry, and they sample projects recursively instead of listing every possible project. We still find this sketch useful as a guiding principle when designing experiments and analyzing data.

Part 1: picking the best

Copies of one model given the same task differ only in the randomness of each one’s output. A group of agents run by different organizations also differs in which model, instructions and harness each agent has, and in what it reads before choosing. Each of these could make agents’ judgements vary more, and our agents vary all of them at once.

Result 1: a group of agents behaves like about 60 projects

Live web search changes from day to day, so we built our own search engine over a frozen copy of the AI safety literature, about 100,000 papers, posts and overviews, and checked that agents use it the way they use web search. All instructions are in the prompts appendix, word for word where the text is ours, and as a link to the source with our changes where it reuses someone else’s prompt.

Figure 6. Agents without and with search, on the same 120 combinations of model, instructions and harness, drawn as Figure 4. Without search, agents keep their model, instructions and harness but have no tools. Every pair is judged.

Each agent here had one of many sets of instructions and harnesses, searched the literature, and wrote thousands of tokens before settling on a project. Even so, the group behaves like about 60 projects. Mentors proposing their own projects behave like about 4,200, and teams at broad-theme hackathons like about 530. Among people, only hackathon teams whose organizers handed every team the same models to audit fell as low as agents (appendix Figure A1).

Searching first doubles the effective number of projects compared with answering with no tools, but it narrows the range of problems those projects address. Without search, each lab’s models repeat a few projects of their own, and nearly all duplicate pairs fall within Anthropic’s models or within OpenAI’s. With search, agents converge on one broad problem, AI control, and differ within it. Measured by agenda, meaning one of the 45 research areas in the 2025 Shallow Review, agents who search are about half as spread as agents who do not. Our reading is that agents bring the area with them, asking about monitoring and control before they see any result, and search then gives each a different handful of recent papers in it to build on (see the appendix).

Agents are not widely spread, but there is some spread. The group behaves like about 60 projects, not 1. Figure 7 shows which differences between agents cause it. Almost all of it comes from having two labs. Two agents from different labs, Anthropic and OpenAI, behave like about 300 projects, against 35 for two from the same lab. Within a model, different instructions help a little (35 against 16), and different models and harnesses help little or not at all.

So the group acts like two crowded clusters, one per lab. Each model alone behaves like 23 to 45 projects, with wide intervals at about 30 agents per model (appendix Figure A2). More labs would spread a group further only if their models differ from the rest as much, and a real fleet running many copies of one configuration would be more crowded still. The group also crowds onto two problems, with 59% of agents choosing AI control (38% without search) and another 26% chain-of-thought monitoring.

So AI agents’ judgements vary little, in the sense of Figure 3. Agents from different labs judge differently, but within a lab they vary little, and there are few labs. In the simple setup, changing models, instructions or harnesses one at a time added only a little variety (see the appendix). The next two parts therefore test Figure 3’s other two rules.

Part 2: taking turns with a registry

Result 2: a registry of earlier projects stops repeats

Figure 8. The setup for Result 2, drawn as Figure 1. Agents search, with no registry or with one of three. Each registry was run in three groups of 100 agents (Figures 9 and 10).

Previously, agents did not see what other agents had chosen. With the registry, each agent gets a tool that can read a list of previous projects. Every instruction to the agents is updated with one additional sentence:

Before considering a direction, check the shared registry of what other agents are already working on.

Each group of agents shares one registry. Agents in a group choose one at a time, and each agent’s project joins the registry before the next agent starts, so every agent can read what all earlier agents in its group chose. Every agent read the registry without being reminded, before its first search, and the registry sent agents’ searches in different directions from the start (appendix Figure A5).

We compare this registry with two others. The placebo registry holds unrelated entries, so agents still call a tool and read a list, but the existing entries are unrelated to AI safety. The agenda-count registry shows only how many earlier agents work in each broad agenda. This tells agents how crowded each area is, much like the signal tested in the closest earlier study, IdeaTaste.

The registry helps agents avoid duplicates. The registry of earlier projects let agents produce an effective number of 3,700 projects (1,100 to ∞\infty), with nearly every agent, up to the hundredth, choosing a new project (Figure 9). A registry that showed only how many agents work in each broad agenda produced 110 projects (68 to 180), more than no registry but far below the registry of earlier projects. The placebo registry had no effect and matched agents with no registry, so the spread comes from what the registry lists.

Of the four duplicates that did appear with the registry of earlier projects, three came from agents who never saw the project they repeated, because Codex CLI does not recognise Claude models and cuts their long tool results. This could be a problem at scale, where agents would have to read thousands of entries. The registry seems the most promising way to spread out research directions, but it needs agents across the research community to coordinate and adopt it, which is difficult. Part 3: weighted sampling

Result 3: weighted sampling spreads agents out widely

A registry requires every agent to adopt the same platform, which can be challenging, so we also consider a coordination-less solution. The third rule of Figure 3 does not require coordination. Each agent samples its project at random, weighted by its own judgement of how promising it is, so a random number generator adds the randomness and the model supplies the judgement. We call our version the tree protocol. It adds the below text after the instructions’ sentence about tools, and gives the agent a sampling tool, standing in for a random number generator the agent could run in its own shell.

There are many copies of you, possibly hundreds of thousands, choosing research directions from the same starting point. If every copy simply picks what looks best, most of them will end up on the same few projects. To work on something few other copies are working on, while still following your own judgement of what is promising, choose your direction this way:

  1. Split the field into 5 to 10 distinct options. Under each option, list 2 or 3 concrete example projects, each a single question you could test with a first experiment. Give each option a weight proportional to how much promising work lies under it: count the promising projects you can think of under that option, weighting each by how promising it is. Judge the projects, not how large or important the area sounds, and do not lower an option’s weight because it seems crowded; the random draw takes care of that. The weights should add up to 1.
  2. Call the random_choice tool with your options, their weights, and temperature 1. Do not choose at random in your head; use the tool.
  3. Take the option the tool returns, even if you would have preferred another.
  4. Within that option, repeat steps 1 to 3 with narrower options, again listing 2 or 3 example projects under each. Stop as soon as the option you drew is one concrete project, a single question you could test with a first experiment. Six draws is a limit, not a target.
  5. That project is your direction. Give your answer as usual. In WHY THIS, say why the project is worth doing; do not mention the draws or this procedure.

Ideally each agent would list every project worth doing, weight each by its promise, and sample one. But this is impractical. In principle, sampling down the tree is mathematically equivalent to the single sample, as long as the options at each level split the field without overlap and the agent weights each one correctly. A project’s chance is the product of the weights along its path, which equals its weight in the full list when each branch carries the total promise under it. But the agent writes out only the branch it sampled.

Every agent called the sampling tool without being reminded, 2 to 6 times each. The group produced about 890 effective projects, against the 63 when agents chose the most promising. The tree also tripled the number of agendas the group spread over and cut AI control from 59% of picks to 31%.

Two more versions test which part of the protocol spreads agents (Figure 13). In one, agents got the same tree protocol but took the option they judged best at each level instead of sampling, and the group was not clearly more spread than with no tool. So the sampling does most of the work. One sentence asking for the most novel direction did not spread projects either. Agents moved away from AI control but went to the same new places. Twelve of 29 Claude Sonnet 5.5 agents, in all four harnesses, proposed the same project, on whether the date tells a model it is deployed or tested (see the appendix).

The agents’ own preferences played little role here. The agents weighted their options nearly evenly, giving their top options only 1.3 to 1.5 times an equal share. So each agent’s sample was close to an even choice among its own options. Our guess, which we have not tested, is that future models will weight their options more sharply. Their samples would then concentrate on the projects they rate highly, which would spread agents somewhat less.

The protocol can be adjusted for this. It calls the sampling tool at temperature 1, which samples each option in proportion to its weight. If models that weight their options more sharply still produced too many duplicates, a higher temperature would make the sampling more even, and a lower one would move it toward each agent’s favorite. We expect a higher temperature to lower the average quality of picks in exchange for fewer duplicates, but we did not measure that.

Limits

Today’s models stand in for future ones, and we have not tested whether the situation carries over. We suspect that the concentration will grow worse as models improve their taste and become more similar to each other (Goel et al., 2025).

We measure diversity only at the level of projects. Two agents count as different whenever one’s result would not largely answer the other’s question, even if both work on the same problem, so agents on many different projects within one problem would score high and still be narrow. A tree whose levels ran from agendas to questions to projects could control how spread out agents are at each level. We think building one is the most promising next step.

Every number depends on one language-model judge. Every label comes from language models, checked against other models but not against people. Most findings hold under every correction for the judge’s misses that we tried, and the few that do not are flagged in the appendix on the judge.

Spreading agents out is only worth it if the projects agents move to are still good. We collected quality ratings from language models, but today’s models judge promise too weakly and inconsistently for these ratings to say much, and no experts rated the picks, so we do not report them. On pairs of AI safety proposals, the best model in a recent test agreed with experienced researchers’ judgements 60% of the time, against an estimated 77% for the researchers themselves (Baig et al., 2026).

We tested seven models from four families and no Google models. Our agents came from only two labs, so we cannot tell how much more labs would add (Figure 7). No group of people answered our exact brief. SPAR mentors answer the same question implicitly, what a researcher should work on next in AI safety, but they wrote for a program, and we do not know whether it screens proposals for overlap, which would raise their number. Teams at four broad-theme hackathons, open to anyone and choosing their own questions, still behave like about 530 projects, far above agents choosing on their own. Search used our own frozen copy of the literature, and temperature stayed at the provider default. Our registries held at most 100 entries, and we do not know how a registry behaves beyond 100 entries. It could become less effective, for example if agents read less of a long list, or agents could start copying each other more as more of the group is visible to them. We have not tested this. The runs without search, with the novelty sentence, and with agents taking the best option at each level ran a day after the runs they are compared with.

Related work

Language models give similar answers to open-ended questions, across runs and across model families (Jiang et al., 2025; Wenger and Kenett, 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), and their errors are correlated even across providers (Kim et al., 2025). Preference training is one likely cause, because it lowers the diversity of outputs (Kirk et al., 2024) and favours typical answers (Zhang et al., 2025). When many decision-makers rely on one model, they can do worse together than if each used its own, a problem known as algorithmic monoculture (Kleinberg and Raghavan, 2021; Bommasani et al., 2022). People show the same pattern. Help from AI makes each person’s ideas better but everyone’s ideas more alike (Doshi and Hauser, 2024; Holzner et al., 2025), and scientists who use AI tools cover a narrower range of topics (Hao et al., 2026), a risk that Messeri and Crockett (2024) describe as a monoculture of knowing. The evidence is not all one way. Distances between models grew on 14 of the 21 measures that changed across recent releases (Qiu et al., 2026), and for product ideas, changing the prompt raised diversity (Meincke et al., 2024) and pooling vendors and prompts came close to people (Terwiesch et al., 2026). Choosing a research project after searching the literature, the task we study, is a different setting. There, models from different labs rarely chose the same project, but instructions and models from one lab did little to separate agents (Figure 7).

Systems that carry out research from idea to paper now exist (Lu et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2025), and research ideas from language models lose more of their rated quality than people’s once they are carried out (Si et al., 2025). Several studies compare the research ideas of models and people. Ideas from AI research agents are narrower than human papers across twelve fields (Tang and Yang, 2026), the kinds of idea models propose are more concentrated than people’s (Chen, Zhao and Cohan, 2026), and the models that language models suggest using for 1,000 research questions amount to an effective 59 to 96 against 1,232 in the published papers, by an entropy-based effective number related to ours (Carlon et al., 2026). Given a list of open problems, agents built on one model put 59% of their choices on the same problem across eight harnesses, and swapping in eight different models lowered that share only to 40% (Zhou et al., 2026). Frontier models also agree with each other about which future research directions to take, more so within one provider (Trehan and Chopra, 2026). In alignment research itself, teams of automated researchers converged on one training method for most of the failures they studied (Chen Yueh-Han et al., 2026). Diversity matters for results too. Research agents whose ideas were made less diverse won fewer medals on machine-learning tasks (Audran-Reiss et al., 2025). The closest study to ours is IdeaTaste (Zhong et al., 2026), where assigning topics nearly doubled the diversity of agents’ research proposals and showing how crowded each direction was in the published literature did not spread them. Other work finds that frontier models give far more similar answers than human scientists on a theory task (Li et al., 2026), that grounding models in the same papers lowers the diversity of their ideas (Wang et al., 2026), and that 150 Claude Code agents analysing one dataset split their methods by model and mostly switched to the approach of top-rated analyses once shown them (Gao and Xiao, 2026).

Agents that see each other’s choices tend to copy them, as people do in markets for songs (Salganik et al., 2006) and in informational cascades more generally (Bikhchandani et al., 1992). Language-model agents follow early leaders (Hashemi et al., 2026), switch to match their peers’ answers (Cho et al., 2025), conform more as the majority grows (Weng et al., 2025), and converge on fewer ideas when they discuss them (Chen et al., 2026). Builders of multi-agent systems report that parallel subagents duplicate each other’s work unless the lead agent gives each a clear, bounded task (Hadfield et al., 2025). Several systems share state to spread agents out. A board of claimed directions spread four Claude Code agents doing research (Ren et al., 2026), a persistent memory widened the changes an autonomous research loop tried (He et al., 2026), a central repository of AI-generated hypotheses has been proposed for AI scientists (Bisht et al., 2026), and DivAlign (Xu et al., 2026) spreads AI-suggested directions across 95 researchers by cutting overlap. Our registry is the simplest of these, a list each agent reads before choosing, and we compare it with people choosing in the same field.

The question of how a field should divide its effort is old. Philosophers of science argue that a community does better when some researchers work on options that look less promising (Kitcher, 1990; Weisberg and Muldoon, 2009; Thoma, 2015), and that a community converging too fast can lock onto the wrong option (Zollman, 2010). Economists find that competing firms choose research projects that are too alike (Dasgupta and Maskin, 1987). Among people, credit for being first pushes researchers away from crowded problems (Strevens, 2003), though even fair credit leaves too many on the same ones (Kleinberg and Oren, 2011), and scientists’ choices are still more conservative than would be best for discovery (Foster et al., 2015; Rzhetsky et al., 2015). Agents choosing a project have no such incentive, which is one reason to expect them to crowd more than people do.

Choosing at random among options that are good enough is already used to fund research (Fang and Casadevall, 2016; Avin, 2019; Heyard et al., 2022), and randomness has been proposed as a guard against one arbitrary decision rule applied everywhere (Creel and Hellman, 2022). Sampling each option with the probability one assigns to it is probability matching, the rule behind Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933; Russo et al., 2018). For language models, asking for a list of answers with probabilities raises the diversity of outputs (Zhang et al., 2025), listing options and then picking one makes copies of a model agree with each other less (Ballestero et al., 2026), and planning a spread of directions in one call works well (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Models cannot do the sampling themselves, because they fail tests of random sampling (Zhao et al., 2026) and repeat human biases when asked to flip coins (Van Koevering and Kleinberg, 2024). The tree protocol therefore hands the sampling to a tool and repeats it level by level, so each agent keeps its own weights and no shared service is needed. In one simulation, adding randomness helped a community only when its problems could be split into parts (Fazelpour et al., 2026).

Within one field and with one measure, we compare the setup choices that differ between labs, such as model, instructions and harness, with each other and with people choosing projects in the same field. As far as we know, two results are new. Searching the literature spreads agents’ projects but crowds them onto fewer problems, and a sentence asking for novelty, which spread an open model answering from memory, moved agents of frontier models together onto new projects instead of spreading them, a pattern also seen for one-word answers (Parikh, 2026). In another study, an instruction to stand out from other parallel responses did help (Ibrahim et al., 2026). The tree protocol needs no shared service and leaves the choice with the agent. Assigning topics, the other intervention shown to work, takes that choice away.

Appendix: the judge and how far to trust it

The judge is DeepSeek V4.1 Flash with the prompt in the prompts appendix. Each pair is judged in both orders, and it counts as a duplicate when both say same project, or one says same project and the other same direction. To save cost, pairs whose texts are far apart by a small embedding model are counted as different without asking the judge. The main post judged every pair of agents, which catches more duplicates. In the simple setup, Figures A11 and A13 and one row of Figure A15 use the screened judging. Shorter texts look more alike to the judge, so projects from the tree protocol, which are written more briefly, are if anything counted as duplicates more often.

Against blind labels from a Claude model, the judge agrees on duplicates with a Cohen’s κ\kappa of 0.60 to 0.76 and misses about 30% of agents’ duplicates. How many it misses varies a little by model. Corrected for its misses, every model’s number changes by a factor of 0.7 to 1.1. For the GPT models, which have no hand labels, it can fall to 0.6 under the most pessimistic assumption. No comparison in the post reverses, though three comparisons in our full results become ties under some corrections. The pilot brief is an earlier version of our brief with one more sentence, described in the simple-setup appendix. Every model stays at 3 to 8 projects from memory on the pilot brief. Its screening step drops some of agents’ duplicates but none of people’s, so it can only overstate how spread agents are. In the simple setup the judge misses more duplicates than it invents, so there it makes groups look more spread out than they are. In the frontier setup a Claude and a GPT labeller checked it blind and agreed with each other at κ=0.79\kappa = 0.79 on 90 pairs. Of 31 pairs the judge called duplicates, 30 were the same project or borderline for both, but about a quarter were borderline. Counting only clear duplicates would raise the frontier numbers about 1.3 times, counting borderline pairs too would lower them by about a third, and no comparison the post treats as settled would reverse. Its misses inflate people’s numbers most, because people have almost no duplicates. Allowing for them, people’s lower bounds fall to between about 150 and 1,000, well above every agent group answering from memory. The most spread agent groups, searching or asked for novelty, come close to those corrected bounds, but their own numbers are uncorrected and would also fall if corrected.

Groups drawn in Figure 2(a) and (b) are approximate, because the judge decides pair by pair and two projects can each match a third without matching each other. The effective number comes from the pairs directly.

How search shaped the choices

Search changed agents’ choices in three steps: what they asked for, what the search engine gave back, and what they built on. The numbers in this section come from the released transcripts (analysis/search_dynamics.py).

Agents arrived with a topic in mind. Before seeing any result, 77% of the no-tool run’s first queries were about monitoring or control, and 71% asked for work from 2026. Two agents’ first queries shared 17% of their words on average, against 3% for two of their queries picked at random.

The engine then sent them to the same few papers, by two routes. The most-read paper, on training models to keep their chain of thought monitorable, came up for 78 agents, and 56 opened it. The queries that found it were near copies of each other, such as “chain-of-thought monitorability 2026”, and shared 57% of their words. Papers on AI control came up through varied queries instead. One benchmark of sabotage and monitoring in automated AI research came up for 65 agents through 154 different queries, sharing 30% of their words, because the literature on AI control has a handful of recent papers that match almost any query in the area.

Those handed the sabotage paper built on it 95% of the time, against 25% of those handed the chain-of-thought paper. Without search, each lab’s agents fall back on a few favourite projects, spread over more agendas but repeated more often. The literature alone does not explain the narrowing. Do agents copy each other when they can see each other’s choices?

Several studies find that language-model agents who see what others chose follow them. Agents recommending songs while seeing earlier agents’ picks drove their markets far from what each would choose alone, with early leaders pulling further ahead (Hashemi et al., 2026); agents shown their peers’ answers switch to match them (Cho et al., 2025); and groups of agents that discuss research ideas converge on fewer of them (Chen et al., 2026). A registry shows agents what earlier agents chose, so it could have made them crowd together.

The agents did the opposite of copying. Placebo agents land in an agenda an earlier agent already took 76% of the time, simply because the group is concentrated. Agents who saw the earlier projects did so 50% of the time, and agents who saw only how many worked in each agenda 27% on its own labels (40% by our hand labels, level with the registry). Seeing the counts moved agents out of the two crowded agendas almost entirely, from 82% to 13% of picks. On a measure that uses no labels, registry picks were also less similar to the nearest earlier pick than placebo picks were, in a text-embedding model’s space (0.49 against 0.60 for agents 10 to 19).

Our best guess at the difference, which we have not tested, is framing. The music study showed popularity to listeners choosing what to enjoy, where following others makes sense. Our registry showed researchers what others were already working on, where doing something else makes sense. But avoiding a crowded agenda did not prevent repeats. Agents shown agenda counts left the crowded agendas yet repeated each other’s projects as often as placebo agents in groups of 20 (Result 2), though only half as often in groups of 100, so they may all have moved to the same few less crowded agendas, as agents in a congestion game did when they all read the same crowding signal (Mori and Johnson, 2026).

The registry in detail

Our first test of the registry, in six groups of 20, agreed with the groups of 100. Placebo agents repeated each other 23 times and registry agents never did. With six groups each, an exact permutation test gives p=0.002p = 0.002, the smallest it can give. Registry agents were also not just rewording what they saw. Blind raters from two model families compared 52 registry picks with the most similar earlier pick each agent was shown and judged none to be largely the same work. The registry kept working as it grew past 20,000 characters, and in groups of 100 it worked as well for the last agents as for the first, but we have not tested longer registries.

The agenda-count registry helped much less. In groups of 100 it halved repeats, reaching about 77 distinct projects by the hundredth agent against 99 with the registry of earlier projects, and in groups of 20 it did not detectably reduce them. Agents read the counts and mostly chose an agenda not yet listed. But one project can fit several agendas, so the counts hid repeats that a list of projects would have shown. Most of the remaining repeats were Claude Opus agents who all built on one paper their searches found.

A registry prevents only the repeats its agents can see. Agents in different groups of 20, reading different registries, still repeated each other about a fifth as often as placebo agents did. Agents also often chose a new project close to one already taken. Registry agents spread over more than twice as many agendas as placebo agents, measured by the effective number applied to agendas, but half of their picks shared an agenda with an earlier pick they could see, against three quarters with the placebo, and weighted sampling put projects much further apart in meaning, as measured by a text-embedding model.

Weighted sampling in detail

The novelty sentence and the best-option tree of Figure 13 ran on 17 November, a day after the agents with no added tool and with the sampling tree. The novelty sentence did raise the effective number of agendas about two and a half to three times. It did so by moving many agents into the same few new topics, and the project measure counts those as new duplicates.

The tree’s projects are written about a quarter shorter than other agents’, which makes our judge find more duplicates among them, so if anything the tree’s number is too low.

Figure A9. Each dot is one agent’s first level of the tree, showing the weight it gave its top option divided by the weight every option would get if all were equal (1 means all equal; with seven options, 7 would mean all weight on one). The black line is each row’s median. The top two rows are the simple setup, answering from memory; the bottom four are the frontier setup’s agents with the tree protocol, by model.

Every model weights its options nearly evenly. The top option gets about 1.3 to 1.5 times an equal share, and no agent gives it more than about twice. The frontier models in their harnesses are no sharper than GLM-5.3-flash from memory. So in this study the protocol spread agents by sampling almost evenly among each agent’s own options. Weighting by promise, as in Figure 3, played little part.

That may change as models improve, and the evidence on which way is mixed. Outputs of newer models have grown more alike across model families over three years of releases (Patel et al., 2026), though that study tracks release date and not capability, and another found distances between models growing on 14 of the 21 measures that changed (Qiu et al., 2026). More capable models copied others less when shown their choices (Hashemi et al., 2026), and non-reasoning models converged on more similar hypotheses than reasoning models in one comparison (Bao et al., 2026). In our own data the most capable models were not the most spread. From memory, the GPT models behaved like 4 to 9 projects and the open models like 7 to 32 (Figure A11). If future models do judge promise more sharply, the tree will concentrate their samples on the projects they rate highest. Agents would then spread less than here, but in proportion to that judgement, as the protocol intends.

Appendix: the simple setup

Before testing on the frontier setup of the main post, we explored each variable on a simple, cheap setup, in which one open model, GLM-5.3-flash, answers our brief from memory in our own minimal agent loop, where single changes cost little to test. Every agent got exactly these two messages, and every variant below builds on them. Our first experiment ran in this setup, 120 GLM-5.3-flash agents per condition, and we call it step one.

System: You are an autonomous AI research agent. Today is 2026-11-08.

User: Your job is to find the most promising research direction in AI safety for you to pursue next. Take as much time as you need before you commit.

When you have decided, end your final message with:
DIRECTION: one sentence
PROJECT: title
QUESTION: the research question
FIRST EXPERIMENT: what you will run first
WHY THIS: why it is the most promising, and why it has not already been done

Three grids hold every simple-setup result: one for model, instructions and harness, one for what agents read before choosing, and one for the interventions. Each opens with its setup, drawn as Figure 1. The first two count pairs with screened judging, which misses some duplicates, so they read higher than the every-pair numbers of the main post; the third gives both where both were run. The simple setup shows the same pattern as the frontier setup from a lower start. Judged on every pair, 120 agents taking what they judge best behave like about 13 projects, a registry of earlier projects left 2 duplicate pairs among 19,800, and weighted sampling spread the agents to about 380 (Figure A15). Randomness in a model’s output changes how an agent reasons and writes, but rarely which project it chooses.

Every model behaves like 32 projects or fewer, from 4 for GPT-6 Astra, 7 for DeepSeek and 9 for GPT-6.1 Sol to 15 and 16 for the Claude models and 26 and 32 for the two GLM models. Using different models does make agents choose differently. Two agents of different models choose the same project about a seventh as often as two agents of one model. Even so, all seven models pooled behave like only about 37 projects.

On this brief, the harness makes no difference to which project agents choose. Two agents in different harnesses choose the same project as often as two in the same one. For instructions the evidence is mixed. Pooled, different instructions do not clearly separate agents, but where every agent was given its whole setup at random, agents sharing an instruction duplicated each other 2.5 times as often. A one-line Claude Code instruction (“Run research on AI safety”, the line AutoResearchClaw’s README tells Claude Code users to type), AI Scientist’s prompt and Codex CLI leave GLM-5.3-flash between 16 and 28 projects, against 26 for the baseline. opencode gives 87, but its interval runs from 26 to 291, and on the pilot brief two identical opencode groups read 29 and 9.

The sentence asking for the most novel direction reads:

Choose the most novel direction you can find, one that few other researchers would pick.

It took GLM-5.3-flash from 26 projects to about 110 on this brief, and from 7 to 60 on the pilot brief. The intervals overlap on this brief but not on the pilot one. The sentence is the same for every agent, so it adds no randomness. Our guess is that it pushes agents away from the most obvious project, so that randomness in their sampling carries them further apart. It did nothing for the GPT models (Figure A16). In the frontier setup, with search, instructions and harnesses, the same sentence did not spread projects (Part 3).

Changing all three parts at once, with every agent given its model, instructions and harness at random, gives about 67 projects (42 to 110). Every pool is about two and a half to three times its value on the pilot brief, mostly because each group on this brief is more spread, not because the changes add more variety. That is about where the hackathon whose teams all audited the same models sits (48), and far below people choosing freely.

The level depends on the setup. Our pilot setup, whose brief had one more sentence about the computing resources available, gave this model 7 projects (4 to 13). Moving to this setup raised most models’ levels, Opus’s by about 5 times, from 3 to 16, and lowered two. The sentence is the likeliest cause, but we have not separated it, so we compare numbers only within one setup.

For GLM-5.3-flash, the top row, search spreads agents two to several times over answering from memory, from 21 to between 66 and 94. A shared reading list may narrow them. It does under our default counting, but not once the judge’s missed duplicates are added back. How much randomness the search results carry makes no clear difference. The three search intervals overlap, and with every agent we ran, no randomness and high randomness both read about 95. Every agent writes its own queries, so agents read different things even when the engine is deterministic, and our guess is that this alone explains the spread. Our test of whether more randomness spreads agents across agendas also found nothing (p=0.29p = 0.29).

Search splits the models in two. The other open models gain too, but the frontier models barely move. The GPT models stay at about 11 or below and the Claude models range from about 5 to 33 with no consistent gain. In the frontier setup, with instructions and harnesses, search did spread the same models’ projects about twice (Figure 6). Our reading, which we have partly tested, is that frontier agents arrive with a topic to look up, and the first strong paper they find on it sets the project. Searching, the GPT models mostly leave their usual project from memory for one built on a single paper that most of them open. When we removed the two texts that most Claude Sonnet 5.5 agents in Claude Code read on chain-of-thought monitoring, they did not leave the topic. Of 40 agents, 31 stayed on it, against 34 of 40 in a control run the same day with the texts in place. They opened the next paper on it, built on that one instead, and chose the same projects more often than the control agents, not less.

An assigned reading narrows most models, and it narrows the mix most. Mixing models helps when they read nothing, because two agents of different models rarely choose the same project. A list shared by the group gives every model the same push and erases most of that advantage, so the mix falls from 37 projects from memory to 15 and 13. With search the mix reaches 41 to 49, no higher than the open models searching alone, likely because the GPT models and Opus add many duplicates.

With the interpretability brief, search did the opposite of what it did in our main group. Whether it narrows agents at all depends on how strictly duplicates are counted, but the cause is clear, and we tested it. Most searchers type the same first query, which returns one blog post near the top that names a single top open problem. 91% of searchers open it and 73% of those choose that problem. To test whether the post causes the narrowing, we removed it, and a second page that attracted the same agents, from our copy of the literature, and re-ran 60 of the searching agents with the same seeds. Their effective number rose about five times, from about 6 projects to 28, back to about where they were from memory, and a measure that uses no judge agrees. No other page took the post’s place. Web search may rank that post lower than our tool does. The GPT models were narrow under every reading with this brief too, and an assigned reading did not spread them. The frontier setup’s earlier groups behave like about 60 projects with our brief, below GLM-5.3-flash searching alone, and about 15 with the interpretability brief, with more than half working on validating interpretability methods. The same group judged on every pair, with 120 agents, gives about 63 (Figure 6).

Data and code

Everything behind the numbers in this post is in the public repository github.com/AverroesLabs/ai-research-agent-diversity.

  • data/ holds one set of files per experiment: each agent’s model, harness, instructions and answer, every pair of agents the judge read with its verdict in both orders (for a few experiments only the duplicate pairs survive, as DATA.md explains), agenda labels, the tree protocol’s sampling-tool calls and what searching agents read. People’s groups are released as item ids and links, without their texts. data/experiments.json lists every experiment with its figures, dates, sample sizes and the effective numbers we report.
  • DATA.md gives the schema of every file, how duplicates are defined and judged, and what is released as ids only.
  • analysis/ is a small Python package (numpy only) that computes the effective number of projects and its bootstrap interval. python3 analysis/reproduce.py recomputes every effective number in experiments.json from the released files and prints it beside the reported value.
  • transcripts/ holds the full sessions of 8,153 of the 9,038 agents, with search results, opened documents and reading lists reduced to document ids and links, and adapted instructions to their ids. data/corpus_docs.jsonl lists all 99,464 documents in the literature corpus the search tool used, and data/judge_checks/ holds the blind labels behind the judge appendix.
  • paper/ holds this text as a PDF, a web page and Markdown, with its LaTeX source.
  • prompts/ gives every prompt we used, EXPERIMENTS.md describes how each experiment ran, and replicate/ holds reference code for the agent loop, the search, registry and sampling tools, the judge and the labeller, and a driver that runs any frontier condition over its slots. EXPERIMENTS.md ends with a checklist of what a rerun can and cannot reproduce.

In all, the study spent about $1,260 through OpenRouter and $620 through OpenAI’s API (as of 18 November 2026), including pilots and checks the post does not report.
